## Major evidence conclusions

### Sleep duration and sleep inertia

Controlled chronic sleep-restriction protocols and reviews support the claim
that inadequate prior sleep magnifies post-awakening impairment. Circadian
phase and sleep stage materially alter the response. The candidate therefore
uses a bounded negative direction rather than claiming a universal linear
dose response or a fixed persistence period.

### Circadian phase and chronotype

DLMO and validated chronotype measures covary, but questionnaire preference,
corrected midsleep, and physiological phase are not interchangeable. This
supports a symmetric noncausal association. Timed light and melatonin can
shift endogenous phase, but intervention direction depends on timing. Neither
intervention directly targets Circadian Timing Alignment; the RDS must be
recalculated from phase and external timing requirements.

### Sleep continuity, fatigue, and pain

Experimental fragmentation can increase objective sleepiness and impair some
next-day tasks even when total sleep time is similar, but selected performance
tests do not always change. Prospective pain literature supports bidirectional
sleep-problem/pain relationships with substantial heterogeneity and residual
confounding. It does not yet isolate the exact canonical continuity→persistent
pain proposition strongly enough for review-ready causality.

### Environmental noise

Pooled polysomnographic evidence supports event-related awakenings from
nighttime transportation noise, while self-reported disturbance estimates
depend strongly on whether the question names noise. This supports keeping
average ambient level (`ENV-039`) and event intermittency (`ENV-041`)
conceptually distinct, and limits generalization to other sound sources.

### Intervention effects

- Behavioral sleep extension generally increases duration, but effects are
  highly heterogeneous and intervention descriptions are often incomplete.
- Delayed school starts are associated with longer adolescent sleep, mostly in
  nonrandomized policy changes; transport and after-school schedules are
  important boundaries.
- CBT-I reliably improves self-reported sleep initiation/maintenance and
  insomnia outcomes. Objective PSG/actigraphy effects are smaller or mixed,
  and time in bed/total sleep time may initially decline.
- Light and melatonin have phase-resetting evidence but require exact timing,
  dose/intensity, population, and safety context.
- Acoustic attenuation shows context-specific benefit, particularly in ICUs,
  while alarm audibility and tolerance are nontrivial risks.
- Pre-awakening caffeine has promising formulation-specific evidence from a
  small crossover trial; broad reactive-countermeasure reviews remain
  unconvinced. Post-awakening light has insufficient objective-performance
  support.

## Important conflicts and null evidence

- CBT-I: robust subjective improvement contrasts with absent or adverse
  actigraphy total-sleep-time effects in some meta-analyses.
- Sleep fragmentation: induced fragmentation reliably increases physiological
  sleepiness, but cognitive-task effects differ across experiments and tasks.
- Pain: recent meta-analyses support reciprocal prospective association but
  differ in certainty for pain→sleep versus sleep→pain and use broad exposure
  definitions.
- Light therapy in delayed sleep-wake phase disorder: within-group clinical
  shifts occur, while between-group effects and persistence are inconsistent
  in a small review.
- Sleep-inertia countermeasures: caffeine is most plausible but evidence is
  sparse; light more consistently changes subjective alertness than objective
  performance.
- Shift-schedule modification: conclusions are roster-specific and mostly low
  certainty; “schedule change” is not one homogeneous intervention.

## Generalization constraints

Most controlled sleep-inertia and forced-desynchrony studies use small, healthy
adult samples. School-start evidence is adolescent-specific; ICU acoustic
evidence is setting-specific; CBT-I evidence applies to diagnosed insomnia;
and circadian interventions require phase-aware protocols. Sex, age,
chronotype, baseline sleep need, clinical disorders, drug metabolism, task,
and operational safety can all change outcomes. These limitations are encoded
in effect boundaries rather than hidden behind a single confidence label.
